Epicurus’ proclamation that Nature “has made what is necessary easy to supply, and what is not easy unnecessary” describes more than land-provided sufficiency, but how we can find the key to happiness: by aligning our desires to natural limits, so that they can be satisfied more easily. Before his proclamation, Epicurus claims “even the greatest wealth is not riches but poverty,” and that one could “have a golden couch and a rich table” yet “be full of trouble,” suggesting a lifestyle of asceticism can provide a richer life in contentment and sufficiency than a dependence on superficial luxuries (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 36–37).

If you “sternly reject the harmful” and lead a life of moderation and simplicity, you can become “independent in all things” according to Epicurus (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 36). This idea of finding more by having less resonates not just with the classical thinker, but also ones from the present-day. Cultivating mental peace and bodily health does not require much—just the avoidance of unnecessary pains or disturbances. By doing so, anyone can reach “the aim of the life of blessedness” (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 37).

But what, exactly, does Epicurus consider necessary and unnecessary? He first describes desire as either vain or natural—the former being unnecessary and the later being necessary. Then, of those natural desires, Epicurus continues his description, saying “some are necessary for happiness, some for the repose of the body, and some for life itself” (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 36).

It’s easy to misread Epicurus’ words as a rejection of ambition or effort. However, his advice serves as a caution against losing sight of our true needs. When we forget what is natural and necessary, we “create for [ourselves] unlimited fears and desires,” leading to standards of our own making—like wealth or social status—that continually escalate and are never truly satisfied (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 36). What Epicurus views as most important is whether our efforts, be it economical or social, bring “greater discomfort…as the result of them” (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 36).

Identifying what you need, and self-sufficiently maintaining them, might sound like a difficult task. However, Epicurus said himself about what is necessary being “easy to supply, and what is not easy unnecessary” (Competing Visions: An Introduction to Moral Philosophy, 37). When I reflect on my needs, one of the first things that surfaces in my mind is food. Hunger, as a need, will always be present, and though it is a pain when unmet, it does not take much to satisfy that particular need—nor does it have to be expensive. For example, a bowl of cut fruit tastes just as good, and makes me just as happy, as fine-marbled beef from a steakhouse. Not only does meeting this need satisfy my body, but it also satisfies my mind—hunger is a pain that can bring pleasure.

In conclusion, I agree with Epicurus’ steps to achieving a good life. By choosing which pleasures to avoid, which pains must be taken, and focusing only on what is necessary compared to the superfluous, we break free from the timeless struggle of wanting more and learn to value what we have—which, in the end, opens our eyes to what true wealth really is, and how close it really is. True wealth is peace, within ourselves, and peace, with our place in the world.
